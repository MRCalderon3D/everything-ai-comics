The last ferry left the east dock at ten past eleven, an hour after curfew, with no cars on the deck and no lamp lit except the shielded one over the wheel. The river was wide there and black, and the far bank showed nothing.

Mara had run the night crossing alone for nine years. She was past fifty, with grey hair she cut short herself and a nose that had been broken on a winch handle and set by nobody. She steered with one hand and kept the other in her coat pocket, out of the wind.

The harbour patrol had been on the radio all evening. A boy had gone over the fence at the bonded sheds. Twelve or so, they said. Any vessel that saw him was to signal and hold its position.

She was halfway over when she heard it. It was not the engine and it was not the chain. Something shifted forward, near the two crates lashed at the bow, and then stopped the way a thing stops when it knows it has been heard.

Rats did not weigh that much. She took the flare pistol from its hook by the wheelhouse door, because it was the only thing on board that looked like a weapon, and went down the ladder with it.

The deck was wet and it should have been empty. She walked forward to the chain locker beside the crates and stood there long enough to hear breathing. "I know you're in there," she said.

She lifted the hatch herself. The boy was folded into the locker with his knees under his chin. He had a round face and a hood pulled up, and he held his left hand against his chest with the sleeve dark around it. He had cut it on the fence wire. He looked at the flare pistol and not at her.

"Please," he said, very quietly. "Don't signal them."

Astern, a searchlight had started to work its way across the water. Mara looked at it, and then at the pistol, which would have brought the launch alongside in two minutes. She lowered the hatch until a hand's width of dark was left. "Stay down," she said. "Not a sound."
